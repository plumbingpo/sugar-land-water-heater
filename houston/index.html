Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Houston

Most early failures start with maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Flush the tank every year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and test the relief valve each year so it doesn't seize. Keep the temperature near 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. If you have a tankless unit, descale it yearly where the water is hard so the heat exchanger stays clean.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs like discolored hot water, rumbling during heating, moisture around the base, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and far less stressful than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both modest fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can wear out the valve and stress the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement and a cleanup. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Common Water Heater Problems in Houston

Most water heater problems we see in Houston come back to what's in the water. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling is worth keeping up with in hard-water areas.

Age is the other big factor. Plenty of homes around here still have the original tank, and beyond about ten years, the anode rod has usually been consumed and rust begins attacking the steel itself. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well can see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless unit in hard water should get regular descaling. And an older install getting a new heater often needs code items it never had: an expansion tank, a correctly piped relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. All of these can be fixed, and most are much cheaper when caught early.
